Abstract

After a large-scale disaster such as an earthquake or fire, responders must locate survivors across areas that are too dangerous to search directly or too large to search quickly. Robotic systems could provide this capability at scale, yet full-system development and evaluation in large-scale disaster environments are challenging because realistic physical trials are dangerous and difficult to reproduce.

We present DisasterWorld, a benchmark for multi-target survivor search by a team of robots during the initial response phase. It provides procedurally generated scenes crossing 2 localities (urban and suburban), 4 disaster types (hurricane, tornado, fire, earthquake), and 3 damage levels, each paired with 5 sampled survivor configurations, for 120 released environments in total. Additional survivor configurations can be generated on demand without rebuilding the underlying scene. Scenes include physics-settled debris with active collision geometry and evidence-informed survivor locations, together with exact ground truth for survivors and damaged structures. By enabling repeatable multi-target search missions under a fixed mission budget across this range of settings, DisasterWorld evaluates complete autonomy systems using time-sensitive measures of survivor discovery and path efficiency.

Scene Generation

Each 1 km × 1 km scene begins as a seeded 2-D layout of roads, parcels, buildings, vegetation, vehicles, and public spaces, generated from summary statistics of OpenStreetMap data rather than copied from a single real location. A disaster model decides where damage occurs, the damage is applied to buildings and other scene elements, and physics simulation settles displaced objects and debris before the geometry is fixed. Scenes are assembled from Unreal Engine/Fab and Objaverse-XL assets and run in NVIDIA Isaac Sim, with a ready-to-use aerial-robot configuration built on AirStack.

Disaster Models

Each disaster model defines a spatial damage intensity over an otherwise intact layout. Local intensity is combined with object type, construction, and environmental conditions to assign structural damage states, informed by published damage scales and post-event studies.

  • Fire spreads from an ignition region as a wind-oriented ellipse whose elongation grows with wind, following wildfire-growth models, leaving burned structures, vegetation damage, smoke, and active flames.
  • Tornado damage is concentrated along a meandering track and falls off smoothly across it, with damage categories following the Enhanced Fujita Scale.
  • Hurricane damage is broad wind exposure with small spatial variation, plus inundation behind an irregular flood boundary.
  • Earthquake intensity decreases radially from an epicenter and is combined with construction type and site conditions to produce facade failure, soft-storey failure, collapse, and settlement.

Survivor Placement and Ground Truth

Survivors are added after the damaged geometry is fixed, so new placements can be sampled without rebuilding the scene. Locations, poses, and occlusions are sampled near damaged structures, debris, evacuation routes, and shelters, informed by post-event studies and agency reports. Each task is exported as a USD scene with JSON annotations giving the exact 3-D location, extent, pose, semantic label, and attributes of every survivor and scene element.

Task and Metrics

Within a mission time budget T, a team of ground or aerial robots must visually locate and report as many people as possible. Each robot receives only onboard RGB-D observations and its estimated state; no prior map, scene geometry, survivor count, or survivor location is available. Robots may act independently, communicate, or share a centralized policy. Each detection is reported with a timestamp and estimated world-frame location, and a survivor is credited at the earliest valid report from any robot.

Because finding survivors sooner matters, the primary metric is Time-Integrated Progress (TIP), the normalized area under the curve of survivors found over time:

TIPi = 1 − 1⁄Ni ∑j tij / T

where tij is the time survivor j is first found (T if never). We also report Progress, the fraction of survivors found by the end of the mission, and Progress-weighted Path Length (PPL), which also rewards finding them with shorter routes.

Results

We evaluate 5 search policies spanning systematic coverage, geometric exploration, and semantic object-goal navigation on 24 tasks (one survivor arrangement per scene, 1,122 survivors in total), with teams of 8 aerial robots running AirStack under a 600-second mission budget.

RAVEN achieves the highest TIP and Progress, and Frontier the highest PPL. Every method finds partially obstructed survivors less often than unobstructed ones, and survivors lying on the ground are rarely found. The low scores across the board leave substantial room for improvement in both search planning and multi-robot coordination.
